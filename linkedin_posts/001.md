# Pragmatic AI - Agentic Workflow Series #1: Introduction

Imagine finding a role that looks almost perfect. AI engineering. Interesting company. Relevant experience. Remote. Salary meets your expectations. It has it all.

Then, somewhere near the bottom of the description: "Applicants must reside in Switzerland." I live in the Netherlands. That would have been useful information about six paragraphs ago.

The information was there. You still had to read it and connect it to your circumstances before you could decide whether the role was worth pursuing.

That makes the engineering problem more interesting than filtering by a few fields. You need to interpret what the description says in the context of the person applying, then explain how you reached the recommendation.

**So, how would you design a system that interprets the descriptions while keeping your requirements and execution limits explicit?**

---

**"But isn't that what LinkedIn Jobs does?"**

Well, yes. Kind of.

Job platforms give you filters for titles, locations, employment types, and other attributes. Those are useful starting points. Your circumstances, however, don't always fit neatly into them.

Perhaps you're moving from backend engineering into AI engineering, and your previous titles only tell part of the story. Or you're not ready to apply yet: you're exploring the requirements for your next career move and trying to understand the gaps between your current experience and the roles you want.

You might also have requirements that need a closer reading: whether a company accepts independent contractors, where you can work from, or how much office attendance it expects. A matching title and a few filters only get you part of the way to those answers.

Each employer has its own vocabulary, format, and way of expressing requirements. Some details are explicit. Others are buried, ambiguous, or missing altogether.

Checking a location field against a list of countries is straightforward. Understanding whether a role advertised as "remote across Europe" still requires residence in one particular country means reading the description and connecting its conditions to your circumstances.

**So, purely hypothetically, I built my own agentic workflow for job searching.**

Of course, LinkedIn and other job platforms have rules around scraping and automation. So, for the purposes of this series, let's imagine an entirely fictional project, built by an entirely fictional engineer who also happens to live in the Netherlands.

You provide your profile and criteria. The system searches for opportunities, collects details, evaluates matches, and returns ranked recommendations with explanations. It also highlights gaps between your skills and the job requirements, helping you decide where to focus next.

The goal is to help you spot promising roles, understand why they fit, and see where to focus your development.

Building it raises questions you will recognize from other systems: what does each component own, what data crosses its boundary, and what happens when it returns something unexpected? Adding a model also gives you another question: what if the response is structurally valid but the decision is wrong?

---

**Why I'm calling it an "agentic workflow"**

Let's be fair. "Multi-Agentic System" sounds more impressive. Probably gets more views, too.

When you design a system like this, the terminology should help you understand where decisions belong.

An AI agent is a system that can choose actions toward an objective, observe their results, and adapt what it does next. Its autonomy lies in choosing how to proceed within the capabilities and limits you give it.

In a job search, that could mean inspecting the results of a query, recognizing that they are unsuitable, and choosing different search terms. You define the objective and available tools; the agent decides which action to take next.

A workflow defines the steps that make up the larger process. Some steps may query an LLM, others may execute tools, and others may use ordinary code without an LLM at all. A fixed sequence of those steps does not, by itself, provide the autonomy needed to call the whole system autonomous.

That is why I use **agentic workflow** here: the overall process is defined in code, and it includes agents with room to choose actions within their responsibilities, as you will see throughout the series.

You get flexibility where the language and evidence vary, while keeping execution paths, tool access, and limits explicit. Those boundaries give you specific places to investigate when something goes wrong.

---

Building this workflow gives you a practical way to explore decisions that carry into other AI applications: separating evidence from policy, choosing where to grant autonomy, defining response contracts, and evaluating whether a change improves the result.
